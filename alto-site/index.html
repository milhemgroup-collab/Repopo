Alto Ingredients (ALTO)

The one-page take

Alto is a commodity crush business that has stopped bleeding. Revenue has fallen three years running — $1,223M → $965M → $918M — but gross profit went the other way, $15.7M → $9.7M → $34.9M. The reason is almost entirely one segment: Western Production, the Oregon and Idaho plants, swung from a $51.1M pretax loss in 2024 to a $4.3M loss in 2025 after management cold-idled Magic Valley and stopped marketing Columbia. That single swing is $46.8M of the company's $71.5M pretax improvement. The core Pekin, Illinois campus earned $15.1M pretax and the trading arm another $7.7M.

The asymmetry is carbon, and it arrives in two stages. Stage one is already in the numbers: Section 45Z clean-fuel credits contributed $7.5M in FY2025 at $0.10/gallon, and with indirect land-use change removed from the GREET model management guides to $0.20/gallon and roughly $15M net in 2026 — Q1 already booked $3.9M. Stage two is not in the numbers at all. The Pekin campus vents over 600,000 tonnes of CO2 a year and sells only about 190,000; Columbia produces 120,000 and sells 57,000. Section 45Q now pays $85 a tonne for sequestration. Capture the whole 720,000 and the gross credit is $61M a year against a $360M company. See §05 and §06.

The catch is that the profit is the credit, and the capital hasn't been spent. Strip 45Z and the $6.7M of insurance proceeds out of FY2025 and pretax income is negative $1.5M — the GAAP profit is entirely non-operating. Capex ran $4.6M against $25.2M of depreciation, or 18%: the reported $8.6M of free cash flow is borrowed from the asset base, and at maintenance capex the number is minus $12M. Meanwhile the sequestration option that makes the bull case work needs pipelines, Class VI wells and years of permitting that nobody has priced — and in May the company registered a $150M shelf, 42% of its own market cap. See §02 and §08.

Why the mix matters

Two-thirds of revenue is a commodity: fuel-grade ethanol whose margin is the crush spread — corn in, alcohol out — and which management does not control. The other third is where the business actually differentiates. Pekin's specialty alcohols go into mouthwash, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals and beverage-grade spirits at a premium to fuel; the essential-ingredients line sells dried yeast, gluten meal and distillers grains into animal feed.

The tell is that revenue fell 25% over three years while gross profit more than doubled. Alto is deliberately selling less — it idled a plant and stopped chasing low-margin gallons. Judge this company on gross profit per segment, not on the top line.

Read the income statement carefully — three things sit below the operating line

  • Section 45Z is not a tax line. Alto accounts for its clean-fuel credits under IAS 20 as a government grant, recognising $7.5M net of selling costs as "Transferable tax credits, net" — below income from operations, above pretax. It is real, transferable and saleable for cash. It is not operating margin, and it expires 31 December 2029 unless Congress acts.
  • $6.7M of the profit was an insurance claim. The Pekin loading dock was damaged in April 2025; gross proceeds were $10.0M, of which $6.7M was booked as excess insurance proceeds. That does not recur.
  • Do the subtraction. Income from operations $7.4M, less interest $10.8M, plus other income $1.9M, gives −$1.5M. Every dollar of FY2025's $13.3M net income — and more — came from the credit and the claim.

Bottom line on the multiple: cheap on the tape, not cheap on the assets

At ~8.4x forward EV/EBITDA and 1.47x book, Alto does not look expensive next to a specialty-chemicals peer group — and the TTM figures show the underlying business crossing into genuine profitability, +$10.6M pretax before any credits. That is a real change from FY2025's −$1.5M.

But the 42x P/FCF is the number to sit with. Free cash flow of $8.6M exists only because capex was $4.6M against $25.2M of depreciation. You cannot run five plants at 18% of depreciation indefinitely, and you certainly cannot build carbon-capture infrastructure at that rate. Charge maintenance capex at D&A and free cash flow is negative $12M. The stock is priced as though the turnaround is done and the carbon option is free; the balance sheet says the capital cycle hasn't started.

The cyclicality, in one row

Look at 2024: gross profit of $9.7M on $965M of sales — a 1.0% gross margin — a $51.7M operating loss and $24.8M of impairments. Nothing about the business model changed between 2024 and 2025 except crush margins, which went from $0.08 to $0.23 per gallon in the fourth quarter, and a decision to stop running the worst plant.

That is the drawdown you are underwriting. A corn-alcohol producer's margin is the spread between two commodities it does not control, and the spread has been negative before. Everything else in this dossier — 45Z, 45Q, specialty mix — is a modifier on top of that spread, not a replacement for it.

04

The 45Z engine

The clean-fuel production credit is the one part of the bull case that is already banked, already audited, and already doubling.

Section 45Z of the Inflation Reduction Act pays producers per gallon of qualifying low-carbon transportation fuel sold, with the credit scaled to the fuel's carbon intensity against a statutory baseline. Alto's Columbia plant and the Pekin dry mill qualify; the Pekin wet mill and the ICP plant do not.

  • FY2025: $7.5M recognised, net of monetisation costs — about $0.10 per gallon, booked in the fourth quarter for the full year.
  • 2026 guidance: with indirect land-use change removed from the GREET model, management expects $0.20 per gallon and roughly $15M in total net proceeds across Columbia and the Pekin dry mill.
  • Q1'26 actual: $3.9M booked in the quarter — an annualised $15.6M, tracking slightly ahead of guidance.
  • Volume: approximately 90 million gallons of combined annual production qualifies.
  • Auditor treatment: RSM flagged the credits as a critical audit matter, verifying clean-fuel producer registrations, agreeing emissions rates to third-party certifications and testing 45ZCF-GREET model inputs.

Why this line deserves a different multiple than the crush spread

45Z revenue is a function of gallons produced and measured carbon intensity — not of the corn-to-ethanol spread. It is close to pure margin: no incremental cost of goods, only monetisation costs already netted out. In a year when crush margins collapse, the credit keeps paying.

Two things stop it being a growth story. It is capped by qualifying volume — roughly 90 million gallons, which does not grow unless Alto lowers carbon scores at the wet mill and ICP or restarts Magic Valley. And it has a hard expiry: 31 December 2029. Whatever multiple you assign this stream, it is a four-year annuity with legislative risk, not a perpetuity. Management is explicit that it continues to pursue lower carbon scores to widen eligibility.

05

The 45Q option — 473,000 tonnes nobody is paying for

This is the part that is not in the numbers at all. Fermentation produces a nearly pure CO2 stream — the cheapest carbon in industry to capture — and Alto vents most of its own.

The Inflation Reduction Act, as amended by the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, raised the Section 45Q credit to $85 per tonne for carbon capture, utilisation and storage. Alto also bought Kodiak Carbonic during 2025 for $7.3M net of cash — a CO2 business that management says has "materially changed our calculus" on whether to sell the Western plants at all.

Size the option honestly

Ethanol CO2 is the best capture feedstock there is: the fermentation off-gas is already about 99% pure, so there is no expensive separation step. That is why ethanol plants are the first movers in US carbon sequestration. The credit at $85/tonne on 473,000 unmonetised tonnes is roughly $40M a year — more than the company's entire FY2025 EBITDA.

And yet: Alto has not built any of it. The 10-K language is "assessing" and "developing plans," not constructing. Sequestration needs compression, a pipeline or rail route, an EPA Class VI injection permit — a process that has historically taken years — and capital that a company spending $4.6M a year on capex does not currently deploy. Treat this as a free option with a real strike price, not a revenue line. The single metric to track is a Class VI permit application or a definitive sequestration agreement at Pekin. Until one appears, value Alto on the crush spread plus 45Z.

Takeaway: the option is worth more than the company, which is exactly why it should be discounted hard

At the top of the grid, $61M a year is 17% of today's market capitalisation and roughly 1.9x FY2025 EBITDA — annually, for a credit with a twelve-year life per facility. If Alto sequestered everything at the statutory rate, the credit alone would be worth a multiple of the current enterprise value. That is what the market is flirting with when a $0.92 stock trades at $4.64.

Three disciplines on that number. The capture and compression plant is not free — peer projects run to hundreds of millions, and Alto's entire market cap is $360M. The timeline is permit-bound, not management-bound. And roughly 247,000 tonnes a year are already sold to food and beverage customers at a commercial price, so the incremental credit applies to the unsold 473,000, not the full 720,000, unless those contracts are displaced. A sensible base case assigns this section a probability well under one and no cash flow before the back half of the decade.

Verdict: same thesis, opposite balance sheets, and the difference is who has already paid

Green Plains has spent the money. Carbon equipment liabilities went from $17.9M to $104.2M in one year, interest expense more than doubled to $76.7M, and the company lost $121.3M. It is further down the sequestration road and carries $217M of net debt to prove it. If carbon capture in corn ethanol works, GPRE owns more of the finished asset.

Alto has not spent it. It is profitable, carries a sixth of the net debt, trades at a lower EV/sales — and its carbon story is still a paragraph in the strategy section. That is the cleaner balance sheet and the weaker option.

So the pairing is not "one is better." It is a choice about where you want the risk: GPRE has execution and leverage risk on an asset that exists; ALTO has financing and timing risk on an asset that does not. Alto's $150M shelf (§08) is the market's clue about which of those two positions it is about to move toward. An investor who believes the sequestration thesis and wants it already capitalised should look hard at GPRE despite the losses; one who wants the crush-spread recovery and 45Z annuity with an unpriced carbon kicker on top is describing ALTO.

Share count has been close to static: weighted-average basic shares were 73.3M in 2023, 73.5M in 2024, 74.5M in 2025 and 74.8M in Q1'26. Diluted was 76.6M in Q1'26. Historically this management has not been a serial diluter — it bought stock back in 2025.

Why the shelf is the single most important item on this page

A $150M shelf filed three months after the stock quintupled, by a company with $23M of cash, $4.6M of annual capex and an explicitly stated ambition to build large-scale CO2 capture, is not a routine housekeeping filing. It is the financing plan for §05.

Read constructively, this is exactly right: raise equity when the stock is up 404% and use it to fund a project whose credit value exceeds the company's EBITDA. Read defensively, issuing $150M against a $360M market cap is up to 40% dilution, and every scenario in §06 has to be run on the post-raise share count, not today's 77.5M. The shelf does not obligate the company to issue anything — but a management team that files one, gets it effective inside two weeks, and is publicly "developing plans to capture more value for our CO2 as rapidly as possible" has told you what it intends to do.

09

Open questions for the research

What this pass could not resolve, ordered by how much each would move the valuation.

  1. What does the shelf get spent on, and when?$150M against a $360M company is the dominant variable. Is it carbon-capture capex, a Magic Valley restart, refinancing, or opportunistic equity with no committed use? Any prospectus supplement filed off this shelf reprices every scenario in §06.
  2. What does Pekin sequestration actually cost?No capture capex, no timeline and no Class VI permit status has been disclosed. Peer projects run into the hundreds of millions. Without a capex number, §05 and §06 are unfalsifiable. Check for a Class VI application at EPA Region 5 and for any sequestration or offtake agreement in the next 8-K.
  3. What is normalised crush margin through a cycle?Q4'25 was $0.23/gallon versus $0.08 a year earlier. What is the mid-cycle number, and what does a 2024-style year — 1.0% gross margin, $51.7M operating loss — look like on the post-turnaround cost base with 45Z running?
  4. What happens after 2029?45Z expires 31 December 2029. It is roughly $15M a year, and on the TTM numbers it is more than the entire underlying pretax profit. Is there a credible extension case, and what is the business worth with the credit set to zero?
  5. Why did Pekin gross profit fall 17% in a year when everything else improved?Pekin gross profit went $23.2M → $19.4M even as segment pretax income rose to $15.1M. Was that mix, the loading-dock outage, or wet-mill economics deteriorating? Pekin is two-thirds of the company.
  6. Sell Magic Valley or restart it?Management is explicitly weighing both, and the restart case is built on 45Z eligibility plus CO2 monetisation. A restart consumes capital and adds qualifying gallons; a sale brings cash and shrinks the 45Q option. This is a live fork.
  7. Can capex stay at 18% of depreciation?$4.6M against $25.2M of D&A. Which is right — that the asset base is over-depreciated and genuine maintenance is far lower, or that FY2025 free cash flow was borrowed from future reliability? The FY2026 capex line answers it.
  8. What are the insiders actually doing?Nine Form 4 filings across April and June 2026, unparsed here. Buy or sell, size, and 10b5-1 status after a 404% move is meaningful signal. Short interest should be sourced to a citable provider at the same time.
